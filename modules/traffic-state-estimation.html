Research brief

In this module you will

  • combine the LWR model with detector data by nudging and by physics-informed deep learning
  • design a study comparing estimators fairly
Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Traffic managers need to know density and speed along every kilometre of a road, every minute. But loop detectors sit hundreds of metres or kilometres apart, and probe vehicles give scattered samples. Filling the gaps is called traffic state estimation. The simplest approach draws straight lines between detectors, but traffic is not smooth: a jam has a sharp front, a shock wave, that moves upstream. Straight lines blur it or miss it entirely. Physics can help. The LWR model says vehicles are conserved and that flow depends on density through the fundamental diagram, so jams propagate as waves at predictable speeds. A physics-informed estimator runs this model and keeps correcting it towards the detector readings, like the Kalman filter from Go 1. Physics-informed deep learning, as in work by Shi, Mo, Di and colleagues, does the same with a neural network whose loss includes the LWR residual. In our ten kilometre ring road with two detectors, interpolation is off by about eleven vehicles per kilometre, and the physics-informed estimate by under two. In this module you will choose how many detectors to install and how much to trust them.

The estimation problem

Unknown: density ρ(x, t) along the road. Known: noisy observations at detector positions and probe locations, and a traffic model with uncertain parameters (free-flow speed, jam density, capacity). Goal: the best estimate of ρ everywhere, with uncertainty, in real time. This is a classic inverse problem, and an ideal test case for SciML (Seo et al. 2017 review).

Model-based fusion

Run the LWR model (Godunov / cell transmission scheme, module 2.3) and at each step pull the cells near detectors towards the readings: ρ ← ρ + g (z − ρ). In the explorer after 10 minutes on a 10 km ring road with a jam: with 2 detectors, interpolation error ≈ 11.4 veh/km versus ≈ 1.9 for the physics-informed estimate; with 5 detectors, ≈ 5.7 versus ≈ 1.1. The model carries information about the jam between detectors. The Kalman filter (and its ensemble version, EnKF) chooses the gain g optimally.

Try it

Set the gain to 0: the physics model alone never learns about the jam. Raise it slowly. Is more trust in detectors always better?

Physics-informed deep learning

Physics-informed deep learning (PIDL) trains a network ρθ(x, t) with loss = data misfit at sensors + λ × LWR residual at collocation points, sometimes also learning the fundamental diagram with a second network (Shi et al. 2021). Strengths: flexible, can learn unknown parameters, no grid. Weaknesses: shock waves are hard for smooth networks; training can be slow for real-time use. Hybrid approaches use the network offline and a filter online.

Designing a fair study

Use a ground truth (high-resolution trajectories such as NGSIM or highD, or a microsimulation), hold out some detectors for testing, compare against interpolation and a Kalman-filter baseline, report errors in congested and free-flow conditions separately, and test robustness to detector failure and parameter error.

Research angle

Open question: how few detectors are needed if physics is used well? This has direct consequences for sensor investment budgets.

Research notes

  • Traffic state estimation fills gaps between sparse detectors and probes.
  • Interpolation misses shock waves; the LWR model carries them between sensors.
  • Model-based fusion (nudging, Kalman filters) and PIDL combine physics and data.
  • Fair studies need ground truth, held-out sensors and congested-condition errors.

New words

Traffic state estimation
Inferring density, speed and flow everywhere on a road from sparse data.
Shock wave
A sharp, moving boundary between traffic states, such as the back of a jam.
Nudging
Pulling a model's state towards observations at each time step.
Physics-informed deep learning (PIDL)
Training networks with physics residuals in the loss, here for traffic.
Ensemble Kalman filter
A Kalman filter variant using an ensemble of model runs for nonlinear systems.
