Spoken overview
Read the transcript

You have now met the main tools of scientific machine learning and seen them at work in traffic, vehicles, safety, energy, climate models, crowds and digital twins. Many questions remain open. Can large foundation models, trained on huge collections of simulations and sensor data, learn general physical behaviour that transfers to new cities and new problems? Can learned world models simulate traffic well enough to train automated vehicles safely? How do we verify that a learned controller will never do something dangerous in a situation it has never seen? How do we model rare events, such as crashes, crushes and extreme weather, when data on them are almost absent? Who benefits and who is left out when models trained in one place are used in another? Answering these needs good research practice. Always compare against strong classical baselines and simple models. Report uncertainty, not just a single error. Test on conditions the model has not seen. State the limits clearly, and share code and data. In this module you will turn these ideas into a checklist for your own project.

This diagram needs JavaScript. Five research fronts, and the reporting habits that make SciML results trustworthy.

Foundation and world models

Foundation models pretrained on huge amounts of physical data (for example, weather models such as GraphCast and Aurora) now rival traditional forecasting systems. In transport, open questions include pretrained traffic models that transfer between cities, and learned world models that generate realistic traffic scenes for training automated vehicles. Do they respect physics, and how do we know when they are wrong?

Safety, rare events and verification

Learned controllers must be verified: reachability analysis, safety filters (control barrier functions) and runtime monitors that fall back to physics-based control. Rare events need simulation, physics constraints and extreme value methods, because data barely exist. Benchmarks with honest rare-event test sets are scarce and valuable.

Pick your front

Choose one front for your project and say which open question you address, which data and physics you use, and how you will know if you are wrong.

Fairness and transfer

Most training data come from a few well-instrumented countries and cities. Models may perform worse in places with different vehicles, road users, cultures or climates, including many cities in the Global South. Check performance by region and group, involve local stakeholders, and prefer physics-based structure that transfers better than pure correlations.

A checklist for honest SciML

Before submitting: (1) a precise question and metric; (2) leakage-free splits; (3) strong classical and simple baselines; (4) physics consistency checks; (5) uncertainty with calibration evidence; (6) out-of-distribution tests; (7) ablations showing what the physics adds; (8) computational cost compared fairly; (9) limits and failure cases stated; (10) code, data and seeds shared. McGreivy and Hakim (2024) found weak baselines common in ML-for-PDE papers: do better.

Common trap

Claiming a PINN 'beats' a solver after tuning the PINN for weeks and the solver for minutes. Compare at equal effort, accuracy or cost.

Reading list

New words

Foundation model
A large model pretrained on broad data and adapted to many tasks.
World model
A learned model that simulates how an environment evolves.
Safety filter
A mechanism that modifies a controller's actions to keep the system safe.
Ablation study
Removing parts of a model to measure their contribution.
Out-of-distribution test
Evaluation on conditions unlike the training data.
