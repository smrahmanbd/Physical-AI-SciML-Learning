Road safety: conflicts, time to collision and rare events

Crashes are rare, so safety studies use near misses. Physics gives surrogate measures such as time to collision; machine learning detects conflicts in video at scale; together they estimate risk before crashes happen.

Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Road safety has a data problem. Crashes are thankfully rare, so waiting for enough of them to judge a new junction design takes years, and every data point is a tragedy. The alternative is to study near misses, also called traffic conflicts. Physics defines them precisely. Time to collision is the gap between two vehicles divided by their closing speed: how many seconds until impact if nobody changes course. A minimum time to collision below about one and a half seconds is a serious conflict. The deceleration rate to avoid a crash, called DRAC, measures how hard someone must brake. Machine learning makes this practical at scale: object detectors and trackers turn hours of traffic video into vehicle trajectories, and the physics measures are computed automatically. Conflicts happen far more often than crashes, and in our simulation the site with shorter gaps has both more conflicts and more crashes. Extreme value theory then links the frequency of severe conflicts to the probability of crashes. In this module you will change gap, speed, reaction and braking and watch time to collision drop.

Time to collision and DRAC

For a follower closing on a leader: TTC = gap / (vfollower − vleader) while closing. DRAC = (closing speed)² / (2 × gap), the constant deceleration needed to avoid impact. In the explorer, a 40 m gap closing at 8 m/s with a 1.5 s reaction and 4 m/s² braking reaches a minimum TTC of 3.5 s (safe); with a 20 m gap the minimum TTC is 0.29 s and DRAC is 4 m/s² (serious conflict).

Why conflicts

The safety pyramid (Hydén 1987): many undisturbed passages, fewer slight conflicts, fewer serious conflicts, very few crashes. In the lab's simulation of 6,000 encounters, conflicts with TTC below 3 s are 10 to 55 times more frequent than crashes, and the site with shorter gaps has more of both (TTC < 3 s: 7% → 19%; crashes: 0.13% → 1.9%). Conflicts give statistical power in days instead of years.

Try it

Keep the gap at 40 m and raise the reaction time from 1.5 to 3 s. When does the outcome change from safe to conflict to crash?

Machine learning at scale

Deep-learning detectors and multi-object trackers extract trajectories from video; physics computes TTC and PET on every interaction; anomaly detection flags unusual manoeuvres. Errors in tracking propagate into the measures, so trajectory smoothing and validation against manual annotation are essential. Physics-informed trajectory reconstruction (vehicles cannot teleport or accelerate at 3 g) removes tracking artefacts.

From conflicts to crash risk

Extreme value theory models the tail of conflict severity (for example, the distribution of negative TTC or of required deceleration beyond a threshold) and extrapolates to the point where a crash occurs. It requires careful threshold choice and validation against crash records; simple tails can be badly wrong. Bayesian hierarchical versions pool across sites (Zheng, Sayed and colleagues). This is an active research area.

Research angle

Open question: which surrogate measure (TTC, PET, DRAC or learned risk scores) best predicts crashes for vulnerable road users such as pedestrians and cyclists?

Research notes

  • Crashes are rare, so safety studies use conflicts measured by physics.
  • TTC = gap/closing speed; DRAC = closing speed²/(2 gap).
  • Computer vision extracts trajectories at scale; physics-informed smoothing removes artefacts.
  • Extreme value models link conflict severity to crash risk but need careful validation.

Reading list

New words

Traffic conflict
An event in which road users would collide unless one changes course or speed.
Time to collision (TTC)
Time until impact if current speeds and paths continue.
Post-encroachment time (PET)
Time between one road user leaving a conflict point and another arriving.
DRAC
Deceleration rate needed to avoid a crash.
Extreme value theory
Statistics of rare, extreme events, used to link conflicts to crashes.
