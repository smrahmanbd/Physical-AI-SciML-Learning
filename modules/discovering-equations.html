Read the transcript

So far we assumed we knew the law. Sometimes we do not. Equation discovery tries to find it from data. The SINDy method, short for sparse identification of nonlinear dynamics, by Brunton, Proctor and Kutz, rests on a simple observation: most physical laws contain only a few terms. So we measure the state, estimate its time derivatives, and build a library of candidate terms, such as a constant, x, v, x squared, x times v and x cubed. Then we fit the derivative as a combination of all candidates, set the small coefficients to zero, and refit, repeating until only a few terms remain. The result is a readable equation, not a black box. For a damped oscillator, SINDy recovers dv over dt equals minus four x minus zero point three v. Noise is the enemy, because differentiation amplifies it, so we smooth first and choose the threshold carefully. A neural ODE, by Chen and colleagues in 2018, takes the opposite approach: it learns the right-hand side with a network, which is flexible but not readable. In this module you will discover the oscillator's law and see when noise breaks it.

The SINDy idea

Write the unknown law as a sparse combination of candidates: dv/dt = Θ(x, v) ξ, where Θ holds library terms as columns and ξ is mostly zero. Sparsity reflects the principle that physical laws are usually simple (parsimony). The method was introduced by Brunton, Proctor and Kutz (2016) and is available in the PySINDy library.

Sequential thresholded least squares

Algorithm (STLSQ): (1) solve least squares for all terms; (2) set coefficients with |ξ| below a threshold λ to zero; (3) refit using only the remaining terms; (4) repeat until nothing changes. In the lab, with noise 0.01 and λ = 0.1, it finds −4.00 x − 0.30 v exactly. With λ = 0.5 it deletes the damping term (−3.89 x); with λ = 0.01 junk terms such as 0.02 x³ survive.

Try it

Raise the noise to 0.05. Can you find a threshold that still recovers exactly two terms?

Noise and derivatives

Differentiation turns small noise into large errors, because it divides differences by a small Δt. Defences: smooth the data before differencing (as in the lab), use total-variation regularised derivatives, or use the weak (integral) form of SINDy, which avoids derivatives altogether. Always check a discovered law by simulating it and comparing with held-out data.

Neural ODEs

A neural ODE (Chen et al. 2018) models dx/dt = fθ(x, t) with a network and trains it by integrating with an ODE solver and comparing trajectories with data. It handles irregular sampling and complex dynamics, but the learned f is a black box. Universal differential equations (Rackauckas et al. 2020) combine the two: known physics plus a small network for the unknown part, which SINDy can then turn into a formula.

Research angle

Discovering traffic or crowd dynamics from trajectory data with SINDy is an active area: can we find car-following laws directly from vehicle trajectories?

Remember

  • SINDy finds a law as a sparse combination of candidate terms.
  • Sequential thresholded least squares fits, cuts small terms and refits.
  • Noise and the threshold decide whether the right terms survive.
  • Neural ODEs learn the right-hand side with a network; universal DEs combine both.

Words and links

New words

SINDy
Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics: discovering equations by sparse regression on a library of terms.
Candidate library
The set of possible terms an equation might contain.
Sparse regression
Regression that drives most coefficients to exactly zero.
Neural ODE
A differential equation whose right-hand side is a neural network.
Universal differential equation
A model combining known physics with a neural network for unknown terms.
