Physical AI: sense, estimate, decide, act

Physical AI closes a loop: sensors measure the world, filters estimate its state, models predict, controllers act, and the world responds. Physics-based estimation such as the Kalman filter is the foundation.

Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Physical AI is artificial intelligence with a body. An automated vehicle, a delivery robot or an adaptive traffic signal must repeat a loop many times a second: sense the world, estimate its true state, decide what to do, and act. Sensors are noisy. GPS jumps by several metres, radar misses targets, cameras are fooled by glare. So the first job is estimation. The Kalman filter, invented in 1960 and used in the Apollo missions, is the classic tool. It predicts where the vehicle should be using physics, position plus speed times time, and then corrects that prediction with the measurement, weighting each by how much it trusts it. If it trusts the model too much, it lags behind real changes. If it trusts the sensor too much, it just follows the noise. The same predict-and-correct idea powers digital twins, which keep a model of a road or a city in step with live data. Because Physical AI acts in the real world, safety, uncertainty and physics constraints are essential. In this module you will tune a Kalman filter tracking a car.

The loop

Every Physical AI system runs a loop: sense → estimate → decide → act. An adaptive cruise control does this about 10 to 100 times a second: radar measures the gap, a filter estimates the gap and relative speed, a controller chooses an acceleration, the engine and brakes apply it, and the gap changes.

The Kalman filter

The Kalman filter (Kalman 1960) alternates two steps. Predict: use physics, xnew = x + v Δt, and grow the uncertainty. Update: compare with the measurement and move the estimate towards it by the Kalman gain K, which is large when the sensor is trusted and small when the model is trusted. In the explorer, with GPS noise of 8 m, a well-tuned filter cuts the error to about 4.7 m; trusting the model too much (tiny q) makes it lag (7.7 m).

Digital twins

A digital twin is a model kept continuously in step with a real system by data, using the same predict-and-correct idea at larger scale. A traffic digital twin of a corridor runs a traffic-flow model and corrects it with detector and probe data, so operators can see the current state, forecast the next minutes and test actions safely. You will build one in module 3.7.

Safety and trust

A wrong prediction in Physical AI can cause a crash. Systems therefore need uncertainty estimates (how sure is the estimate?), hard physical constraints (a car cannot decelerate faster than its tyres allow), fallbacks when sensors fail, and testing in simulation before the road. Standards such as ISO 26262 and ISO 21448 (SOTIF) cover functional safety of automated vehicles.

Research angle

Combining learned perception (neural networks on camera images) with physics-based filtering and control is a major research direction for automated vehicles and robots.

Words and links

New words

Physical AI
AI that perceives and acts in the physical world.
State estimation
Inferring the true state of a system from noisy measurements and a model.
Kalman filter
An algorithm that optimally blends model predictions and measurements for linear systems with Gaussian noise.
Kalman gain
The weight given to a new measurement relative to the prediction.
Digital twin
A model continuously updated with data from the real system it represents.
