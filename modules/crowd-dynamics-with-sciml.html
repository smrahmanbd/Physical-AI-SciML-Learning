Spoken overview
Read the transcript

At mass gatherings, from stadiums to the Hajj, crowd safety depends on predicting what happens at high densities. Yet most measurements come from comfortable conditions, because dangerous densities are rare and hard to measure. This is exactly the setting where pure data fits fail. Suppose we measure walking speed at densities up to two and a half persons per square metre and fit a flexible cubic curve. At five persons per square metre, the cubic predicts people walking faster than at low density, which is physically absurd. A physics-shaped law, such as Weidmann's fundamental diagram, where speed falls to zero at a jam density, needs only its three parameters fitted, and extrapolates almost perfectly. The same idea scales up: learning the parameters of social-force or other pedestrian models from trajectory data, physics-informed networks for crowd density fields, and hybrid route-choice models. Physical AI enters too, through camera-based crowd counting and real-time digital twins of event spaces, which raise privacy questions. In this module you will see a free curve fail where a physics-shaped one succeeds.

Extrapolating to danger

Weidmann (1993): v(ρ) = v₀ (1 − exp(−γ (1/ρ − 1/ρmax))), with v₀ = 1.34 m/s, γ = 1.913, ρmax = 5.4 persons/m². In the explorer, with data only up to 2.5 persons/m², a cubic fit is off by about 2.6 m/s beyond the data (it predicts people speeding up at high density), while fitting only Weidmann's three parameters is off by about 0.01 m/s. With data only up to 1.5 persons/m², the cubic error exceeds 15 m/s; the physics-shaped fit stays near 0.015.

Learning pedestrian models

Social-force and similar models have parameters (desired speed, interaction strength and range, relaxation time) that vary by culture, purpose and setting. SciML calibrates them from trajectories by minimising simulated-versus-observed differences, or learns residual interaction forces with neural networks while keeping the physical structure (Kreiss et al. on social LSTMs; physics-informed hybrids). Equation discovery (module 2.4) has been used to find pedestrian interaction laws from data.

Try it

Lower the highest observed density to 1.5 persons/m². What does the cubic predict at 5 persons/m²? Does the physics-shaped fit change much?

Monitoring with Physical AI

Crowd-counting networks estimate density maps from camera images; optical flow gives velocities; crowd pressure (density × velocity variance, Helbing et al. 2007) flags turbulence. Physics-informed post-processing enforces conservation between camera views. Digital twins of venues combine these with simulation to forecast density minutes ahead. Our Crowd Dynamics course covers the crowd science in depth.

Ethics

Crowd monitoring must protect privacy: use aggregate density and flow rather than identities, process video on site, minimise retention, and be transparent about purpose. Models trained on one culture or venue may not transfer; validate locally, and never let a model replace trained stewards and clear authority to act.

Research notes

  • Crowd data are plentiful at safe densities and scarce at dangerous ones.
  • Physics-shaped laws such as Weidmann's extrapolate safely; free curves do not.
  • SciML calibrates and corrects pedestrian models from trajectory data.
  • Camera-based monitoring needs physics checks and strong privacy safeguards.

New words

Fundamental diagram (crowds)
The relation between pedestrian density, speed and flow.
Weidmann's law
A widely used pedestrian speed–density relation with three parameters.
Social force model
A pedestrian model in which people respond to attractive and repulsive forces.
Crowd counting
Estimating the number or density of people from images.
Crowd pressure
Density times velocity variance; an indicator of crowd turbulence.
