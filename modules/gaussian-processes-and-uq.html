Spoken overview
Read the transcript

A prediction without an error bar is half an answer. Gaussian processes give both. Think of a Gaussian process as a cloud of possible smooth curves. Before seeing data, the cloud is wide. Each observation pins the curves near that point, and the cloud narrows there. Between observations it stays narrow if they are close and widens if they are far apart. The kernel decides what the curves look like. The squared-exponential kernel says two inputs are similar if they are within about one length scale of each other. A short length scale gives wiggly curves that forget quickly; a long one gives smooth curves that can be overconfident. Instead of guessing, we choose the length scale that makes the data most probable, called maximising the marginal likelihood. The test of good uncertainty is calibration: a 95 percent band should contain the truth about 95 percent of the time. Gaussian processes are the workhorse for surrogates and emulators, Bayesian calibration of models, and deciding where to measure next. In this module you will tune a Gaussian process and check its calibration.

A distribution over functions

A Gaussian process (GP) says any finite set of function values is jointly Gaussian, with covariance given by a kernel. Conditioning on data gives closed-form predictions:

μ(x*) = k*ᵀ (K + σₙ² I)⁻¹ y, σ²(x*) = k(x*, x*) − k*ᵀ (K + σₙ² I)⁻¹ k*

The variance does not depend on y: uncertainty shrinks wherever there are observations, whatever their values.

Length scale and calibration

In the explorer with 8 observations: ℓ = 0.6 gives an error of about 0.35 with the truth inside the band almost everywhere; ℓ = 3 is far too smooth (error 1.0, band covers only 19% of the truth: overconfident); ℓ = 0.1 is wiggly and its band is wide almost everywhere (honest but less useful). Good uncertainty is both calibrated (covers as claimed) and sharp (as narrow as possible).

Tuning by marginal likelihood

The log marginal likelihood, log p(y | ℓ, σ), balances data fit against complexity automatically. In the lab, scanning ℓ from 0.1 to 3 and picking the maximum gives ℓ ≈ 0.8. Libraries (scikit-learn GaussianProcessRegressor, GPyTorch) optimise it by gradient methods.

Where GPs are used

Emulators of slow simulators (module 3.5, GCAM); Bayesian calibration of model parameters with a model-discrepancy term (Kennedy and O'Hagan 2001); Bayesian optimisation, choosing the next experiment where improvement is likely; and spatial interpolation of sensor data (kriging). Their cost grows with the cube of the number of points, so sparse approximations are used beyond a few thousand points.

Words and links

New words

Gaussian process
A distribution over functions in which any finite set of values is jointly Gaussian.
Kernel
A function measuring similarity between inputs; it defines a GP's covariance.
Length scale
The distance over which a kernel considers inputs similar.
Marginal likelihood
The probability of the data given the hyperparameters, averaging over functions.
Calibration
Agreement between stated and actual coverage of uncertainty intervals.
