Differential equations: the language of physics

A differential equation says how something changes. Solving it, with a formula or step by step with methods such as Euler's, predicts how the system evolves. Step size controls accuracy.

Read the transcript

Most laws of physics are written as differential equations. A differential equation does not say what a quantity is; it says how fast it changes. For example, a pollutant that decays at a rate proportional to how much is left follows dy over dt equals minus k times y. A queue that grows when arrivals beat departures follows a similar rule. To predict the future, we solve the equation. Sometimes there is a formula, such as an exponential decay. Usually there is not, so we step forward in time: take the current value, compute the rate of change, and move a small step. This is Euler's method, the simplest numerical solver. Small steps are accurate but slow; big steps are fast but wrong, and for some equations they even become unstable. Equations with one variable changing in time are ordinary differential equations, or ODEs. Equations that change in time and space, such as traffic waves or heat, are partial differential equations, or PDEs. In this module you will solve three equations step by step.

A rule for change

An ordinary differential equation (ODE) gives the rate of change of a quantity as a function of the quantity itself. Three examples you will use:

  • Decay: dy/dt = −k y. Each second a fixed fraction disappears (pollutants, radioactivity, fading signals).
  • Logistic growth: dy/dt = k y (1 − y). Growth that slows near a limit (adoption of electric cars).
  • Oscillator: d²x/dt² = −ω² x. A spring, a swaying bridge, a car suspension.

Euler's method by hand

Take the decay law with k = 0.5 and y(0) = 1, step Δt = 0.5 s:

y₁ = y₀ + Δt × (−k y₀) = 1 − 0.5 × 0.5 × 1 = 0.75

Then y₂ = 0.75 − 0.25 × 0.75 = 0.5625. The exact answer at t = 1 s is e−0.5 ≈ 0.607, so the error after two steps is about 0.045. Halve the step and the error roughly halves: Euler's method is first-order accurate.

In the explorer, choose the oscillator and increase Δt. Watch Euler's swing grow. Then make Δt small: does the growth vanish, or just slow down?

Stability and better solvers

For the oscillator, explicit Euler adds a little energy every step, so the swing grows forever. Large steps make things worse, and for stiff problems the solution can explode. Higher-order methods such as Runge–Kutta (RK4) sample the slope four times per step and are far more accurate for the same work; adaptive solvers choose the step size automatically. Every SciML method in this course rests on solvers like these.

Common trap

A solver that 'runs' is not necessarily right. Always halve the step and check that the answer barely changes.

PDEs in transport

When a quantity varies along a road or through space, we get a partial differential equation. The LWR traffic model (Lighthill, Whitham and Richards) says vehicles are conserved: ∂ρ/∂t + ∂(ρv)/∂x = 0. The heat equation, air-pollution dispersion and the shallow-water equations behind flood models are PDEs too. You will solve one in Go 2 and use LWR in Go 3.

Remember

  • A differential equation is a rule for change.
  • Euler's method follows the rule in small steps: y + Δt × slope.
  • Smaller steps are more accurate; large steps can be unstable.
  • PDEs describe change in space and time, such as traffic waves and heat.

Words and links

New words

Ordinary differential equation (ODE)
An equation for how a quantity changes with one variable, usually time.
Partial differential equation (PDE)
An equation for how a quantity changes in more than one variable, such as space and time.
Euler's method
The simplest step-by-step solver: new value = old value + step × rate.
Time step
The size of each step a solver takes.
Initial condition
The value of the quantity at the start.
