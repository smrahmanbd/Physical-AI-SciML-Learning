Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Now we build a physics-informed neural network ourselves, step by step. Our problem: a quantity decays as dy over dt equals minus k y, we have six noisy measurements in the first second, and we want the whole curve to five seconds. Step one, define the network: one or two hidden layers of tanh neurons mapping time to y. Step two, choose collocation points, say forty times spread over zero to five seconds. Step three, compute the residual: the network's derivative with respect to time plus k times its output. In PyTorch, one call to autograd gives the derivative. Step four, build the loss: data error, plus the physics residual, plus the initial condition. Step five, train with the Adam optimiser and plot both loss terms; if one stays high, rebalance. Step six, the payoff: make k itself a trainable parameter, and the PINN discovers the decay rate from the data. Our NumPy version, with hand-derived gradients, recovers k as 0.798 when the true value is 0.8. In the explorer you will watch the two losses fall during training.

Steps 1–3: network, points, residual

In PyTorch:

net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(1,32), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(32,32), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(32,1))
tc = torch.linspace(0,5,40).reshape(-1,1).requires_grad_(True)
y = net(tc); dy = torch.autograd.grad(y, tc, torch.ones_like(y), create_graph=True)[0]
residual = dy + k*y

create_graph=True lets the optimiser differentiate through the derivative. Use smooth activations (tanh, not ReLU), because ReLU's second derivative is zero.

Step 4–5: loss and training

loss = mse(net(td), yd) + lam*mean(residual**2) + mse(net(0), 1), then loss.backward(); opt.step() with torch.optim.Adam(..., lr=1e-3). Plot the data and physics losses separately on a log scale. Healthy training: both fall. Data loss stuck high: physics weight too big or network too small. Physics loss stuck high: weight too small or too few collocation points. Many practitioners finish with the L-BFGS optimiser for extra accuracy.

Try it

Set λ = 10⁻³ and train. Which loss stays high? Now try λ = 10². What happens to the data loss?

Step 6: the inverse problem

Make k trainable: k = nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(0.3)) and add it to the optimiser. Now the PINN looks for a network and a k such that the data fit and the law holds. In the NumPy lab (hand-derived gradients, 16 neurons, 8,000 Adam steps) k moves from 0.3 to 0.798, and the error beyond the data is under 0.001. This is how PINNs estimate unknown physical parameters, such as a road's jam density, from sparse measurements.

Research angle

Inverse problems are where PINNs beat classical methods most clearly: they fuse sparse data and physics to estimate parameters that cannot be measured directly.

Common failure modes

Unscaled inputs: scale t and y to about −1…1. Imbalanced losses: rescale or use adaptive weights. Long time ranges or sharp fronts: PINNs struggle (spectral bias, causality violations); split the domain or train sequentially in time. Too few collocation points: the law is not enforced between them. Always compare with a classical solver when one exists.

The same PINN in PyTorchPythonDownload
"""2.2 The same PINN in PyTorch: automatic differentiation replaces the hand-derived gradients.
Install: pip install torch. Runs on a laptop CPU in under a minute. Also learns k (inverse problem)."""
import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)
K_TRUE = 0.8
td = torch.linspace(0, 1, 6).reshape(-1, 1); yd = torch.exp(-K_TRUE*td) + 0.01*torch.randn_like(td)
tc = torch.linspace(0, 5, 40).reshape(-1, 1).requires_grad_(True)       # collocation points
net = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(1, 32), torch.nn.Tanh(), torch.nn.Linear(32, 32), torch.nn.Tanh(), torch.nn.Linear(32, 1))
k = torch.nn.Parameter(torch.tensor(0.3))                               # unknown decay rate
opt = torch.optim.Adam(list(net.parameters()) + [k], lr=1e-3)
for epoch in range(1, 6001):
    opt.zero_grad()
    y = net(tc)
    dy = torch.autograd.grad(y, tc, torch.ones_like(y), create_graph=True)[0]   # dy/dt by autodiff
    loss_phys = ((dy + k*y)**2).mean()
    loss_data = ((net(td) - yd)**2).mean()
    loss_ic = ((net(torch.zeros(1, 1)) - 1)**2).mean()
    loss = loss_data + loss_phys + loss_ic
    loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if epoch % 2000 == 0:
        print(f"epoch {epoch}: data {loss_data.item():.2e}, physics {loss_phys.item():.2e}, k = {k.item():.3f}")
tt = torch.linspace(1, 5, 50).reshape(-1, 1)
with torch.no_grad():
    print(f"error beyond the data: {torch.sqrt(((net(tt) - torch.exp(-K_TRUE*tt))**2).mean()).item():.4f}")
Autodiff replaces the hand-derived gradients. Requires pip install torch; about 10 seconds on a laptop CPU.

Remember

  • A PINN needs a smooth network, collocation points, an autodiff residual and a composite loss.
  • Train with Adam and monitor data and physics losses separately.
  • Making a physical parameter trainable turns a PINN into an inverse-problem solver.
  • Scale inputs, balance losses and compare with classical solvers.

Words and links

New words

Residual
The amount by which the network breaks the governing equation at a point.
Adam
An adaptive gradient-based optimiser widely used for training PINNs.
L-BFGS
A quasi-Newton optimiser often used to refine PINN training.
Spectral bias
The tendency of networks to learn smooth, low-frequency features first.
Inverse problem
Estimating unknown parameters of a model from data.
