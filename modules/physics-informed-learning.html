Physics-informed learning: the core idea

A physics-informed model is trained to fit the data and to obey a known equation at many points. The physics fills the gaps between and beyond measurements, so far fewer data are needed.

Read the transcript

This is the central idea of the course. Suppose we know the law that governs a system, say that a quantity decays as dy over dt equals minus k y, but we have only a few noisy measurements, all from the first second. A plain network fitted to those points can do anything afterwards. A physics-informed model adds a second term to the loss: at many chosen times, called collocation points, it checks how badly the model breaks the equation, and penalises that. The model must now fit the data and obey the law. Because collocation points cost nothing, we can place them everywhere, including where we have no data, and the physics fills the gap. This is the idea behind physics-informed neural networks, or PINNs, introduced by Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis in 2019. The balance between the two loss terms matters: too little physics and the model wanders; too much and it ignores the data. In this module you will turn the physics weight up and down and watch the prediction lock onto the law.

Two terms in the loss

For the law dy/dt = −k y, a physics-informed loss is

L = (1/Nd) Σ (ŷ(tᵢ) − yᵢ)² + λ (1/Nc) Σ (dŷ/dt(tc) + k ŷ(tc))²

The first term fits Nd measurements; the second checks the law at Nc collocation points. Initial or boundary conditions can be added as extra data-like terms.

Why it needs fewer data

In the explorer, six measurements from 0 to 1 s alone leave the model free to do anything from 1 to 5 s (error about 1.6). Add 20 collocation points with λ = 1 and the error beyond the data falls to about 0.02: the law has replaced the missing measurements. With only 3 collocation points the gaps between them are unconstrained again (error about 0.8).

Try it

Start at λ = 10⁻⁴, then step it up. At what value does the prediction beyond the data snap onto the true curve?

Balancing the terms

If λ is tiny, the physics is ignored. If λ is huge, the optimiser satisfies the law but may drift from the data (in the explorer, λ = 100 raises the error to about 0.17, because the noisy data and the initial value are underweighted). In practice the terms are scaled, or adaptive weighting schemes are used (Wang, Teng and Perdikaris 2021).

Strengths and limits of PINNs

Strengths: work with sparse, noisy data; no mesh needed; can solve inverse problems (find unknown parameters such as k from data). Limits: training can be slow and fragile, especially for sharp fronts such as shock waves; for simple forward problems classical solvers are usually faster and more accurate. PINNs shine when data and physics must be combined.

Research angle

Physics-informed deep learning for traffic (Shi et al. 2021) and for crowds builds on exactly this loss. You will apply it in Go 3.

Remember

  • A physics-informed loss adds a penalty for breaking the governing equation.
  • Collocation points are free, so physics can fill gaps where there are no data.
  • The physics weight λ must balance data and law.
  • PINNs suit sparse data and inverse problems; classical solvers are better for simple forward problems.

Words and links

New words

Physics-informed neural network (PINN)
A neural network trained with a loss that includes the residual of a governing equation.
Residual
How much a candidate solution fails to satisfy an equation.
Collocation point
A point where the equation residual is evaluated during training.
Physics weight
The factor λ that balances the physics term against the data term.
Inverse problem
Inferring unknown parameters or inputs of a model from observations.
