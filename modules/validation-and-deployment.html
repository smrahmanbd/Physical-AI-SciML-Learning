Spoken overview
Read the transcript

A model is not finished when its training loss is low. Before anyone relies on it, we need four kinds of evidence. First, accuracy on truly unseen conditions: a different week, another city, a holiday. Second, physical consistency: does it conserve vehicles, keep speeds positive, respect capacity? Third, honest uncertainty: every prediction should come with an interval that contains the truth as often as it claims. Split conformal prediction is a simple, powerful way to get such intervals. Take a calibration set the model has not seen, compute its errors, and use the error that is beaten ninety percent of the time as a plus-or-minus band. The guarantee holds on average for any model, but not necessarily for every kind of input: when the noise grows with the input, the band is too wide for small inputs and too narrow for large ones. Fourth, a deployment plan: speed, monitoring for data drift, retraining, and a safe fallback when the model is unsure or sensors fail. In this module you will build conformal intervals and see where they fall short.

Validation beyond one number

Report errors separately for normal days, peaks, incidents and holidays; for each site; and for future periods. Test out of distribution on purpose. Add physical checks: count how often predictions break conservation or bounds. A model with slightly higher average error but no physical violations is often the safer choice.

Split conformal prediction

(1) Train the model. (2) On a separate calibration set of n points, compute scores sᵢ = |yᵢ − ŷᵢ|. (3) Take q = the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉-th smallest score. (4) Predict ŷ ± q. For a new exchangeable point, P(y inside) ≥ 1 − α on average (Vovk et al. 2005; Angelopoulos and Bates 2023). In the lab with 1,000 calibration points, a 90% target gives 90.5% coverage.

Try it

Use only 10 calibration examples, then 500. How much does the achieved coverage wobble around the target? Look at large inputs.

Average is not conditional

The guarantee is marginal: averaged over all inputs. When noise grows with x, the same ±q band over-covers small x (about 100%) and under-covers large x (about 80%). Remedies: conformalised quantile regression, normalised scores (divide by a predicted spread), or calibrating separately by group. Always check coverage in the subgroups that matter, such as peak hours.

Deployment

Decide latency (an automated vehicle needs milliseconds; a planning study can wait), hardware (edge device or cloud), and monitoring: track input distributions and live errors for drift, retrain on a schedule, and define a fallback (pure physics model, last good estimate, or a human) when uncertainty is high or sensors fail. Publish a model card stating scope, data and limits (Mitchell et al. 2019).

Common trap

Calibrating on the training set gives intervals that are far too narrow, because the model has already fitted those points.

Remember

  • Validate on unseen conditions and check physical consistency, not just one error number.
  • Split conformal prediction gives distribution-free intervals with average coverage guarantees.
  • Average coverage can hide under-coverage in important subgroups.
  • Deploy with monitoring for drift, retraining, fallbacks and a model card.

Words and links

New words

Conformal prediction
A method that turns any model's errors on calibration data into prediction intervals with coverage guarantees.
Marginal coverage
Coverage averaged over all inputs.
Conditional coverage
Coverage for a specific input or subgroup.
Data drift
A change in the distribution of inputs or outcomes after deployment.
Model card
A short document stating a model's intended use, data, performance and limits.
