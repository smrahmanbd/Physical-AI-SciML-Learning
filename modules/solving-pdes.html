Solving PDEs: classical solvers and PINNs

Partial differential equations are solved on grids by finite differences or volumes, with stability limits on the time step. PINNs solve them without grids. Learn both, and when each wins.

Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Many systems vary in space as well as time: temperature in a pavement, pollution along a street, traffic density along a road. They obey partial differential equations. The classical way to solve them is to cut space and time into a grid. For the heat equation, the rate of change at a point is proportional to how much its neighbours differ from it, so each step updates every grid point from its neighbours. This explicit finite-difference scheme is simple, but it has a stability limit: the number r, the diffusivity times the time step divided by the square of the grid spacing, must stay at or below one half. Cross it and small wiggles grow until the solution explodes. For traffic, conservation laws are solved with finite-volume schemes such as Godunov's. A PINN takes a different route: it needs no grid, only collocation points in space and time, plus boundary and initial conditions in the loss. For simple forward problems a classical solver is usually faster and more accurate, but PINNs handle odd geometries, missing information and inverse problems. In this module you will push a solver past its stability limit.

Finite differences for heat

Approximate the second derivative by neighbours: ∂²u/∂x² ≈ (ui+1 − 2ui + ui−1)/Δx². With an Euler step in time:

uin+1 = uin + r (ui+1n − 2uin + ui−1n), r = αΔt/Δx²

A hot point loses heat to cooler neighbours; a cold point gains. Ends are held at zero (boundary conditions).

Stability: the r ≤ 0.5 rule

The explicit scheme is stable only for r ≤ 0.5 (von Neumann analysis). In the lab with 41 points: r = 0.25 and r = 0.5 give errors below 0.0004, r = 0.52 gives 0.12, and r = 0.6 explodes to 107. Halving Δx forces a quarter of the time step, which is why implicit schemes (Crank–Nicolson) are used for fine grids.

Try it

Set r = 0.49, then 0.51. Look closely at the profile: where do the first wiggles appear?

Traffic: finite volumes

Conservation laws such as the LWR traffic model have shock waves (sudden jams), where finite differences oscillate. Finite-volume schemes track how many vehicles cross each cell boundary; Godunov's scheme (in traffic, the cell transmission model of Daganzo 1994) takes the smaller of upstream demand and downstream supply. Module 3.1 uses it as the physics inside a traffic state estimator. Its time step obeys the CFL condition: vfΔt/Δx ≤ 1.

A PINN for the heat equation

Network u(x, t); loss = mean (ut − uxx)² at 1,000 random points, plus boundary terms u(0,t)² + u(1,t)², plus initial term (u(x,0) − sin πx)². The PyTorch lab trains in about 20 seconds and reaches a maximum error of about 0.04 at t = 0.1, while the finite-difference solver gets below 0.001 in a fraction of a second. For this forward problem the classical solver wins; PINNs earn their place when data must be fused or parameters inferred.

Fair comparison

Papers sometimes compare a carefully tuned PINN with a crude classical solver. Compare at equal accuracy or equal computing time.

"""2.3 A PINN for the heat equation u_t = u_xx on [0,1] x [0,0.1], u(0,t) = u(1,t) = 0, u(x,0) = sin(pi x).
No data inside the domain: physics, boundary and initial conditions only (a forward problem). Install: pip install torch."""
import torch, math
torch.manual_seed(1)
net = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(2, 40), torch.nn.Tanh(), torch.nn.Linear(40, 40), torch.nn.Tanh(), torch.nn.Linear(40, 1))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=2e-3)
for it in range(1, 4001):
    xt = torch.rand(1000, 2) * torch.tensor([1.0, 0.1]); xt.requires_grad_(True)
    u = net(xt); g = torch.autograd.grad(u, xt, torch.ones_like(u), create_graph=True)[0]
    u_xx = torch.autograd.grad(g[:, :1], xt, torch.ones_like(u), create_graph=True)[0][:, :1]
    res = g[:, 1:] - u_xx                                         # u_t - u_xx
    xb = torch.rand(200, 1); tb = 0.1*torch.rand(200, 1)
    loss = (res**2).mean() + (net(torch.cat([torch.zeros_like(tb), tb], 1))**2).mean() + (net(torch.cat([torch.ones_like(tb), tb], 1))**2).mean() \
        + ((net(torch.cat([xb, torch.zeros_like(xb)], 1)) - torch.sin(math.pi*xb))**2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if it % 1000 == 0: print(f"iteration {it}: loss {loss.item():.2e}")
xs = torch.linspace(0, 1, 101).reshape(-1, 1); tt = torch.full_like(xs, 0.1)
with torch.no_grad():
    err = (net(torch.cat([xs, tt], 1)) - math.exp(-math.pi**2*0.1)*torch.sin(math.pi*xs)).abs().max().item()
print(f"max error at t = 0.1: {err:.4f}")
Physics, boundary and initial losses only; no data inside the domain. Requires pip install torch; about 20 seconds.

Remember

  • Grid solvers update each point from its neighbours.
  • Explicit finite differences for heat need r = αΔt/Δx² ≤ 0.5.
  • Finite-volume (Godunov) schemes handle traffic shock waves under a CFL limit.
  • PINNs need no grid; classical solvers usually win simple forward problems.

Words and links

New words

Finite differences
Approximating derivatives by differences between neighbouring grid values.
Finite volumes
Tracking conserved quantities by fluxes across cell boundaries.
Stability condition
A limit on step sizes beyond which numerical errors grow without bound.
CFL condition
Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy limit: information must not travel more than one cell per step.
Boundary condition
A constraint on the solution at the edges of the domain.
