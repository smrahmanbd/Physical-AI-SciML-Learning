Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Every driver follows the car ahead, keeping a gap that depends on speed. Car-following models write this down. The Intelligent Driver Model, by Treiber, Hennecke and Helbing, says a driver accelerates towards a desired speed but brakes as the gap shrinks below a safe distance that grows with speed and closing rate. Its few parameters, desired speed, time gap, maximum acceleration and comfortable braking, have clear physical meaning. Now put ten such drivers in a line and let the leader brake. If each follower reacts a little late and a little harder than the one ahead, the braking wave grows along the line, and the last car may stop completely. This is string instability, the origin of phantom jams. Automated vehicles are Physical AI: adaptive cruise control senses the gap by radar and reacts without delay, and cooperative adaptive cruise control also receives the leader's braking by radio, so it can damp the wave. Learned models can improve on physics, for example by adding a neural residual to the IDM, but must stay safe. In this module you will make a braking wave grow and then tame it.

The Intelligent Driver Model

IDM (Treiber, Hennecke and Helbing 2000):

a = amax [1 − (v/v₀)⁴ − (s*/s)²], s* = s₀ + vT + vΔv / (2√(amax b))

v₀ desired speed, T desired time gap, s₀ minimum gap, b comfortable braking, Δv closing speed. Five interpretable parameters, collision-free in normal conditions, and widely used in simulation and as a baseline for learned models.

String stability

A platoon is string stable if a speed dip shrinks as it travels back. In the explorer, after the leader brakes from 90 to about 40 km/h: human drivers with 0.8 s reaction amplify the dip (factor about 1.8; the last car stops), ACC with T = 1.2 s damps it (factor about 0.5), and cooperative ACC damps it further (about 0.3). Longer time gaps help stability but reduce road capacity: a real trade-off.

Try it

Choose human drivers and increase the time gap from 0.6 to 2.5 s. At what gap does the wave stop growing? What does that cost in road capacity?

Learning car following

Data-driven car-following models (neural networks, Gaussian processes) learn from trajectory data such as NGSIM, highD or Waymo Open. Pure learned models can produce unsafe accelerations in rare situations. Physics-informed options: (1) a residual network on top of IDM; (2) a network predicting IDM parameters by context; (3) training with a loss that penalises IDM deviations (Mo, Shi and Di 2021). All keep collision avoidance and interpretability.

Physical AI on the road

An automated vehicle runs the sense–estimate–decide–act loop of module 1.8 at high rate: learned perception (cameras, lidar), physics-based estimation and prediction, planning and control with safety constraints. Research frontiers include learned world models that simulate traffic, verification of learned controllers, and mixed traffic where a few automated vehicles stabilise many human drivers (Stern et al. 2018).

Research angle

A field experiment (Stern et al. 2018) showed that a single automated vehicle could dampen stop-and-go waves among about 20 human-driven cars on a ring road.

Research notes

  • The Intelligent Driver Model describes car following with five meaningful parameters.
  • Delays and aggressive reactions make platoons string unstable, creating phantom jams.
  • ACC and especially cooperative ACC can damp braking waves.
  • Learned car-following models are safest when combined with physics.

Reading list

New words

Car-following model
A model of how a driver adjusts speed in response to the vehicle ahead.
Intelligent Driver Model (IDM)
A widely used car-following model with five interpretable parameters.
String stability
The property that disturbances shrink as they propagate along a platoon.
Adaptive cruise control (ACC)
An automated system that keeps a set gap to the vehicle ahead using radar.
Cooperative ACC (CACC)
ACC that also uses wireless data from other vehicles.
