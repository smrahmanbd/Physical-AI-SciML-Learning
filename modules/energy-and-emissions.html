Sustainability: vehicle energy and emissions

Vehicle energy follows from physics: mass, acceleration, grade, rolling resistance and drag. Hybrid models add learned corrections for engines and traffic, giving accurate, explainable emission estimates for policies.

Research brief

  • connect vehicle-level models to network and policy analysis
Spoken overview
Read the transcript

Transport produces around a fifth of global carbon dioxide emissions, so estimating vehicle energy use accurately matters for every climate policy. Physics gives most of the answer. The power needed at the wheels is mass times speed times acceleration plus gravity on a grade, plus rolling resistance, plus air drag, which grows with the cube of speed. This road-load equation is behind the vehicle specific power used in the US EPA's MOVES model. A petrol engine turns only about a quarter of its fuel energy into motion, and wastes all braking energy; an electric car is about three times as efficient and recovers part of its braking energy. In our example, an aggressive driving style raises petrol CO₂ by about ten percent, a constant four percent uphill grade roughly doubles it, and the electric car uses about thirteen kilowatt hours per hundred kilometres. What physics misses, such as how engine efficiency varies with load, can be learned from measurements with a hybrid model, which keeps the physics' sound behaviour for new driving styles. In this module you will drive the same trip in different styles and vehicles.

The road-load equation

P = m v (a + g sin θ) + m g Cr v cos θ + ½ ρ CdA v³

For a 1,500 kg car at 20 m/s on the level: rolling ≈ 3.5 kW, drag (CdA = 0.7 m²) ≈ 3.4 kW; accelerating at 1 m/s² adds 30 kW. Vehicle specific power (VSP, Jiménez-Palacios 1999) divides by mass and underlies the US EPA MOVES model's operating modes.

Style, grade and vehicle

In the explorer (synthetic urban trip, about 5 km): petrol car ≈ 138 g CO₂/km; aggressive style (2× acceleration) ≈ 152 g/km; a constant 4% uphill grade ≈ 297 g/km; electric car ≈ 13 kWh/100 km, ≈ 60 g/km at 450 g/kWh grid intensity. Electric cars gain most in stop-and-go traffic because regenerative braking recovers energy that petrol cars waste.

A hybrid emission model

Measured fuel use departs from the simple constant-efficiency model because efficiency varies with load. In the lab, the physics model underestimates CO₂ by about 9% (152 vs 167 g/km); fitting a small correction as a function of power on two driving styles fixes this on an unseen style (167 vs 167) and still helps for a more aggressive one (169 vs 174). The physics keeps the model sensible for new conditions; pure ML trained on one cycle would not be.

From vehicles to policy

Link vehicle models to traffic simulation (link speeds and accelerations) to estimate corridor emissions; to fleet models (EV share, vehicle age) for city scenarios; and to integrated assessment models for national and global pathways. Uncertainty in grid carbon intensity, fleet mix and driving behaviour should be carried through.

GCAM link

GCAM's transport module represents vehicle technologies by energy intensity; better vehicle-level physics and hybrid models improve those inputs.

Research notes

  • Tractive power follows the road-load equation: inertia, grade, rolling and drag.
  • Grade and driving style change emissions strongly; EVs recover braking energy.
  • Hybrid models correct physics with measurements and extrapolate sensibly.
  • Vehicle models scale to networks, fleets and integrated assessment models.

Reading list

New words

Road-load equation
The power needed to overcome inertia, grade, rolling resistance and aerodynamic drag.
Vehicle specific power (VSP)
Tractive power per unit mass, used to classify driving for emission estimation.
Regenerative braking
Recovering kinetic energy during braking to recharge a battery.
Grid carbon intensity
CO₂ emitted per kWh of electricity generated.
MOVES
The US EPA's MOtor Vehicle Emission Simulator.
